Shoreline planning

Point Vernon tides and weather

Last updated: 8 September 2026

By Point Vernon Guide · How we check this guide

The tide does more than move the waterline. It changes how much sand and rock is exposed, how far you may need to walk to the water and which routes across the shore remain sensible.

Before you leave

Where to check today’s tide and Point Vernon weather

Open the official sources below for your visit date. This guide explains the shoreline; it does not display a live tide table or weather reading.

  1. Find the tide prediction. Open Maritime Safety Queensland’s tide tables and select the year of your visit. The 2026 publication includes Urangan as a Hervey Bay standard port. Use the publication’s location guidance and any secondary-location instructions; a nearby port’s times and heights are not an exact observation at your beach.
  2. Check the weather. Open the Bureau of Meteorology’s Point Vernon forecast and review current warnings before heading out.
  3. Choose your shoreline stop. Read the Gatakers Bay tide notes or compare the four Point Vernon beach sections. Allow for changing water along your return route.

One coast, different experiences

What changes between lower and higher tide?

Beach width

Higher water may leave only a narrow strip of beach. Lower water exposes more sand or rock flats.

Rock platforms

Lower tides reveal pools, channels and uneven reef surfaces that are covered later.

Distance to water

At lower tide, shallow water can be a long walk across rock or sand flats.

Return route

A dry route out may become wet, slippery or cut by channels as the tide rises.

Match the visit to the purpose

There is no single “best tide”

  • Rock-platform viewing: a falling or lower tide may expose more, but secure footwear and an easy route back still matter.
  • Shoreline photography: lower water emphasises reef texture; higher water can create cleaner water views. Light and weather may matter as much as tide.
  • A simple beach stop: a higher tide can bring water closer, while leaving less dry beach.
  • Walking: the paved foreshore path is a more dependable route than trying to complete a long walk over tidal flats.

Read the entries before and after your planned visit, so you know whether water is generally rising or falling during the whole outing. Set a turnaround point on land and allow time for the return before the route narrows.

Prediction is not observation

Why the actual water may look different

A tide table is not a depth guarantee

Wind, atmospheric pressure, waves, rainfall, currents, local seabed shape and recent coastal change can alter what you see. Predictions do not tell you whether a specific rock is slippery, a channel is safe to cross or a boat will clear the bottom.

Look at the water and weather when you arrive. Turn back early if the route is becoming harder, and do not continue because an app suggests there should still be time.

Boating needs more information

Tides, ramps and local navigation

Council marks the Aplin Street ramp at The Gables as low-tide restricted. Gatakers Bay is not given that same Council flag, but Maritime Safety Queensland warns of shoals and a need for local knowledge in the Point Vernon area. A tide height alone cannot establish safe ramp or channel access for a particular vessel.

Boaters should use current marine charts, weather, notices, ramp information and their vessel’s safe operating limits. Use the ramp comparison and consider logging on with Marine Rescue Queensland Hervey Bay.

Before stepping onto the shore

A simple tide-and-weather checklist

  • Check official tide predictions and current weather.
  • Notice whether the tide is rising or falling.
  • Choose a clear return route and set a turn-around time.
  • Wear secure footwear on rock and shell.
  • Keep children within reach around water and slippery edges.
  • Stay out of boat-ramp approaches and marked channels.
  • Leave marine animals, shells and habitat undisturbed; marine park rules apply.
