Parraweena Park, Point Vernon

Remnant eucalypt forest

What is Parraweena Park?

Parraweena Park Bushland Reserve lies on the western side of Point Vernon between the mouth of Eli Creek and Eli Point.

Council describes it as a 5.8-hectare reserve with remnant eucalypt forest and habitat for local wildlife. Its current location record points visitors to the corner of Spence and Corser streets.

A family and a sanctuary

The former Parraweena Bird Sanctuary

Council records that the Cook family operated the Parraweena Bird Sanctuary on their own land. They also managed part of the adjoining Council reserve and planted specimen trees and shrubs.

The present reserve combines several pieces of land: 4 hectares of existing Council reserve, 1.1 hectares of the Eaton Street road easement and 0.7 hectares formerly owned by the Cook family and donated to Council. The park took its name from the former sanctuary.

Read the landscape carefully

What remains of the sanctuary?

Council identifies a stone footbridge, two bench seats and the remains of a wishing well as surviving features associated with the sanctuary site.

These are modest traces in a living bushland reserve, not a reconstructed attraction. Vegetation, weathering and maintenance can change how visible they are. Do not move stone, clear vegetation or disturb wildlife to obtain a photograph.

A quiet open-space visit

Planning a visit

Location

Council lists the corner of Spence and Corser streets, Point Vernon.

Facilities

The current Council snapshot lists parks and gardens and open space. It does not list toilets, water, barbecues or a playground.

Expectations

Visit for bushland character, wildlife observation and local history rather than a formal picnic or play facility.

Care

Keep noise low, supervise children, keep dogs under current signed controls and leave natural and built features undisturbed.

What this page does not claim

The Council record establishes the sanctuary, land donation and surviving features. It does not provide a complete operating timeline, visitor numbers, bird list or detailed biography of every person involved.

Community photographs and memories may help future research, but they require provenance, permission and corroboration before being republished as history.
